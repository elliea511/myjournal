# I Wish You Could Have Met Her

I always tell people I wish they could have met my Mawmaw. There is really no good way to explain Barbara to somebody who never knew her. Remembering her is almost always the beginning of a funny story, and everybody who met her remembered her. She could say something completely inappropriate, insulting, or just plain strange, and somehow instead of getting offended, people would laugh and say, “That’s just Barbara.” She was not always nice, but she was so completely herself that people loved even the parts of her that should have been off-putting.
If people think me or any of the women in my family are funny, I always tell them they should have met the woman we got it from. Mawmaw was like a stand-up comedian who occasionally turned on the audience. She was funny. She was mean. And somehow you would have loved her.
She had a saying for everything, although half of them made sense only to her. Something could be “crooked as a dog’s hind leg.” If you were swerving while driving, she would say you were “heading toward Aunt Nancy,” and to this day I have no idea who Aunt Nancy was in that situation or why we were headed toward her. If something was set in stone, it was “like Peter wrote it on a rock,” until Larissa finally told her one day, “Mama, Peter sure did write a lot of stuff on that rock.” If you suggested something she thought was stupid, she did not believe in encouraging you through it. You got a long, drawn-out, “That’s just not going to wooorrrkkk,” or sometimes no words at all. She would close her mouth and make this little humming sound like whatever you had just said was so stupid it caused her physical pain.
She cussed constantly for somebody who worried so much about going to hell. She did try not to sometimes. When we were getting on her nerves, she would warn us in this high-pitched voice, “DON’T MAKE ME CUUUSSS,” which usually meant the cussing was only seconds away. Her religion was very real to her, but there was also something almost childlike about the way she believed. Years before she actually died, she was in the hospital and could not have anything to eat or drink. Katelynn and I were there visiting, and Mawmaw pulled me close with her little dry lips and told me very seriously, “I can’t go to hell. I can never be this thirsty again.”
That was Mawmaw. Somehow even her fear of eternal damnation became funny.
She loved dirty jokes too, which probably did not help her concerns about hell. She could get “tickled” over the most random thing and laugh until she could barely finish what she was trying to say. Usually it started with a little smile and that quiet chuckle because she knew she had said something good. Then eventually she would give in and really laugh. She had almost no sense of embarrassment. She thought farting was hilarious, especially if one of her grandchildren happened to be trapped in bed beside her. She would fart directly on you and then lie there chuckling while you yelled, “MAWMAW!” She would tell you about whatever bowel movement she had that day, flash you while she was changing clothes, joke about her boobs reaching the floor, and make fun of basically every part of herself.
She said she had “the face of a mule.” One time I came home from school with a paper where I had written, “My Mawmaw says she looks like a mule.” She thought that was hilarious. She said her hair looked like a rooster when she woke up and that she was so flat-footed God had just stuck her leg straight into the middle of her foot. Her friend Mary once accidentally called a stick a “dick,” and Mawmaw decided that mistake was funny enough to keep repeating for years, usually while the rest of us wished she would stop.
She was ridiculous, but she was also beautiful. She had naturally bright red hair, a cute little figure, and enough personality for three people. She was very girly. She loved purses, hats, rouge, matching outfits, pretty dishes, antiques, and anything she thought was cute. She bought the same dark reddish-maroon lipstick over and over and would put it on her cheeks too because everybody needed “a little color.” She kept that red hair red for most of her life. Sometimes the hairdresser got a little too enthusiastic and she came home looking closer to Ronald McDonald than she intended, but that never stopped her. She teased those short curls out with an old-fashioned pick and sometimes threw a roller in her bangs.
One time she was staying at our house and did her makeup with different products. I got off the school bus, walked down the hall, turned the corner, saw her, and literally jumped. Somehow she had given herself the darkest black eyebrows imaginable. Mawmaw had pale eyebrows and red hair. Those eyebrows were not right, and I told her immediately. She knew it too, and she laughed about me jumping for years.
There were really only two versions of Mawmaw at home. She was either fully fixed up in the most coordinated outfit anybody had ever seen, usually with a matching hat, or she was in pajamas and a robe under a heating blanket. Yellow went with yellow. Orange went with orange. That was the system.
And if she was in pajamas, she was probably in her room. Her bedroom was the center of the house. The television in the living room was usually off. Everybody went to Mawmaw’s room. That was where she stayed, where she watched TV, where she sang, laughed, fussed, rested, talked on the phone, and held court from the bed. You sat on the edge of it, stretched out beside her, pulled up a chair, or stood in the doorway talking. People came in and out all day.
She had a television schedule that was somehow more rigid than her sleeping schedule. She got up whenever she felt like it, but she was not missing *The Young and the Restless*, even though it pissed her off almost every day. If *The Waltons* was on, everybody needed to leave or be quiet. It did not matter that she had seen every episode enough times to know exactly what happened. She loved *I Love Lucy*, and I still love Lucy because of her. I think she liked Lucy because Lucy reminded her of herself. She loved *Grease*, *Dirty Dancing*, Andy Griffith, *Leave It to Beaver*, game shows, and Steve Harvey, who she absolutely had a crush on. Meanwhile, she thought the host of *Match Game* was “the ugliest man I have ever seen” and acted disgusted every time he flirted or made some suggestive comment, which was pretty rich coming from Barbara.
She loved Ronnie Milsap too. I still feel her when I hear his music. One of my favorite stories is about when she got the chance to meet him. She spent forever planning what she was going to wear. She fixed herself up, picked out the outfit, wanted everything perfect, and then got there and remembered something she had always known: Ronnie Milsap was blind. She had somehow never connected that fact to all the outfit planning until it was too late.
Music was part of who she was. Her family had “pickin’ and grinnins,” where everybody would sit around playing bluegrass. Mawmaw could play piano, guitar, fiddle, mandolin, and probably more than I even know. She could not read music. She just played by ear. There was a beautiful piano in her living room that nobody was allowed to touch unless they were going to play it right, at least until the great-grandchildren came along and suddenly all the old rules disappeared.
She tried to make all of us musical too. When I joined band and played trumpet, she was so proud. She came to football games just to watch me play because finally one of us had turned out musical enough for her. She tried to make singers out of us too, whether we wanted to be singers or not. Once she made me stand beside her in church to sing a call-and-response gospel song. She would sing her part and I was supposed to answer. She sang. I stood there silent. She waited. I still said nothing. Eventually she pinched me so hard trying to make me sing, and I still refused. She was so disappointed in me that day.
That was probably an early sign that I had inherited more Barbara than she was going to enjoy.
Mawmaw was stubborn as a mule and not especially interested in being wrong. It did not take much to piss her off, but there was a difference between irritated Mawmaw and truly mad Mawmaw. When she was really mad, her voice dropped low and her back straightened, and she reminded me of a bull. She was tiny, but anger made her seem twice her size. Once she decided something, very little in this world could move her. Not people. Not Pawpaw. Not even snakes.
Most people would move if a snake came into their fishing spot. Mawmaw would call it a “smart-elic” and refuse because it “was not the boss.” That was the whole argument. The snake was not the boss, so Barbara was staying.
Honestly, that probably explains more about her than anything else I could say.
Nothing was the boss of Barbara if she could help it.
She taught all of us to stand up for ourselves. She taught us not to take any shit. We spent years making fun of how stubborn she was and somehow never noticed that she was leaving behind an entire family full of people with the exact same problem, especially the women.
I understand her more as I get older. I used to think she nagged Pawpaw too much. He was one of the sweetest, quietest men I have ever known, and he worried about everything. His worrying drove her insane. She always complained that all he cared about was what “the fellers at the plant” had to say. She could tell him something ten times, but if one of the fellers at the plant said it once, suddenly it was worth listening to.
I thought she was just aggravating.
Now I live with a sweet, quiet man who can hear me tell him something, ignore it, go to work, hear another man say the exact same thing, and come home to tell me about it like it is brand-new information. Mine are “the fellers at the bank.”
I get it now.
Mawmaw and Pawpaw were fire and ice. She was aggravating and he was annoying, but they loved each other. Every morning before Pawpaw went to work, he would kneel beside her bed and pray with her. Sometimes I happened to be sleeping in that bed too, and it did not matter. He would kneel there, pray with her, stand up, tell her he loved her, and then I would hear his work boots stomping down the hallway.
I think about those boots sometimes. I think about the sounds of that house more than I expected to. The screen door. Cabinet doors opening and closing. Mawmaw singing from her room. People coming in and out. Cuckoo clocks chiming at completely different times because not one of them seemed to know what time it actually was. Her house smelled a little like cigarette smoke because she smoked heavily for years. Sometimes it smelled like coffee in the morning, which I loved. If she had cleaned, it smelled like Pledge. There was always an old antique, thrift-store kind of smell from all the furniture and things she collected.
And she collected everything, purses most of all. She had a closet full of purses and would give one away without thinking, then somehow have three more the next time you came over. QVC and “five easy payments” had a serious hold on that woman. She loved antiques, tea sets, china, old furniture, and weird little things nobody else would think to buy. She had more china cabinets than anybody needed and enough cuckoo clocks to cover the walls. Every closet was full. There was stuff under every bed. If something had even the smallest sentimental value, good luck getting rid of it.
Maybe growing up with very little had something to do with that. Mawmaw grew up poor in Gattman, the youngest of three. She dropped out of school around the fifth or sixth grade and had her first child, my Uncle Emmett, when she was fifteen. Then came Sandy, my mom Tina, and Larissa.
Fifteen.
That age means something different to me now.
When you are little, you do not think about your grandmother as somebody who once had to become herself. She is just Mawmaw. She has always had red hair and opinions. She has always known how to bake, sew, fish, sing, and tell everybody what they are doing wrong. I did not really think about the girl before the grandmother. The girl who loved her brother Bobby and told us the same story a thousand times about how he bought her a doll for Christmas because he “loved her the most.” The girl who loved her daddy. The girl who grew up so poor that she thought Santa Claus hated her.
For years I said Mawmaw hated Christmas, but that was not really true. She loved Christmas trees. She loved colorful lights, nutcrackers, and Christmas candy. She wrapped presents horribly and would wrap the most random little things just so we had more to open. She hated Santa. When she was little, the other kids would come back to school after Christmas and talk about everything Santa had brought them. Mawmaw believed in Santa, and because he brought them presents and brought her nothing, she decided he just did not like her. So naturally she carried a lifelong grudge and talked about shooting Santa Claus right out of the sky.
It was funny because she made it funny, but there was something sad under it too. I think about that little girl watching everybody else have something she did not, and then I think about the grandmother who made sure her house was full of presents, even if half of them were random things wrapped terribly just so we would have more to open.
Maybe some of the things we give people are really things we once needed ourselves.
Her children knew a version of Barbara I did not. They have their own memories of her as a mother, and some of those memories are painful. She was not perfect. She could be selfish, dramatic, difficult, jealous, and exhausting. She could stir up family drama and pit people against each other. She slept a lot. She worried too much about keeping the man she loved. She made mistakes.
She always said she did the best she could.
I used to hear that as an excuse. Now I hear a fifteen-year-old girl saying it. Doing the best you can does not mean your best was enough. It does not mean nobody got hurt. It just means you were working with whatever you had, and Mawmaw had not been given very much to start with.
She loved all four of her children, just differently. My mom, Tina, was almost like her best friend. They were the two redheads and liked so many of the same things. Sandy was probably more like Mawmaw’s twin, which meant they could fight harder than anybody. Emmett was her first child and only boy, so naturally he could do no wrong. Larissa was the baby and fiery in her own way.
I think about Sandy a lot when I think about Mawmaw now. Sandy has said before that sometimes she felt like her mother did not love her. I know Mawmaw did. I also know that being loved and feeling loved are not always the same thing. Sandy and Mawmaw were so much alike that they knew exactly how to fight each other and exactly where to hurt each other. And then, at the end, Sandy stayed.
When Mawmaw became afraid of being alone, Sandy slept in a chair beside her for weeks. There was no big speech that fixed everything. Their history did not suddenly become simple. Sandy just stayed beside her mother and made sure her biggest fear did not come true.
I think there is something beautiful about love that still shows up after it has been hurt.
Mawmaw showed love in her own strange ways too. She absolutely had favorite grandchildren. There is no reason to start lying just because she is gone. Kaleb was her favorite. Everybody knew it. He could do something annoying and it was funny and sweet. The rest of us could do the exact same thing and somehow it was irritating. She liked me pretty good too. If anybody accused her of treating me better, she would say, “You know I’m partial to you,” like that was a defense instead of a confession.
But she thought all of us were better than everybody else’s grandchildren. One time she commented on somebody else’s picture of their grandkids and told them they looked good, but hers looked better. We fussed at her so badly for that. I think that was somewhere around the time we started changing her Facebook passwords, which probably explains why she still has about fifteen abandoned Facebook accounts floating around.
She bragged on us to anybody who would listen. One time an ambulance crew was literally strapping her up to take her to the hospital and she started telling them I made a 36 on the ACT.
I did not make a 36. Or a 35. Or even a 32.
But that was irrelevant.
Mawmaw knew I had done really well, and apparently in her mind that meant 36 was the more accurate way to express how proud she was.
She would call friends just to brag about us. She would slip money into our pockets and tell us not to tell anybody. She would say we could “have anything you want as long as it isn’t poison.” If I needed money, she would tell me to go get her purse, and eventually she taught me how to write myself a check. Even when we were grown and driving ourselves around, she would still call our order in to the Mexican restaurant because we were scared to call people ourselves.
She thought we were the greatest people alive and also apparently incapable of placing a takeout order.
That was her kind of love.
She loved taking care of us, although taking medical advice from her was risky. Mawmaw kept enough pills in her purse to run a small pharmacy. Everybody in the family eventually learned that accepting mystery medication from Barbara was a decision you made at your own risk. Once Sandy was stressed because her ex was going to be at their son’s graduation, and Mawmaw gave her “a pill for her nerves.” Sandy says she spent the rest of the day high as a kite and at one point was walking around Subway looking for headphones she was not even wearing.
Then there was her Phenergan. If you said you were sick to your stomach, Mawmaw would offer you one… four days later when you finally woke up you knew to never accept anything out of her purse ever again.
We all eventually learned the lesson.
Mawmaw loved attention when she was sick. A lot of attention. She could exaggerate a symptom and then refuse every sensible solution anybody offered. One night when I was around twenty, I was asleep on her couch when she woke me up at two in the morning because she thought her blood pressure was high. She asked if my stethoscope was in the car. I told her I was not going out there to get it. She insisted her blood pressure was high. I asked, “Okay, if it is high, are you going to the ER?” She said no. So I told her, “Well then go back to bed and maybe it’ll go back down.”
She balled her fist up at me like she was going to hit me.
I did not back down.
So naturally she went and woke somebody else up crying about how mean I was being to her, leaving out the part where she had just threatened me.
That was Mawmaw too. She could be mean as shit and then have her feelings hurt five minutes later. She did have a soft side. She cried over her daddy. She cried over Bobby after he died. As she got older, she got more emotional when we left. She would look up at us with those big watery eyes and follow us all the way out to the car, still talking, finding another question or another story because one more thing to say might keep us there another minute.
That always makes me think about how she used to hide from us when we showed up unexpectedly. We would stand outside yelling, “MAWMAW! LET US IN!” until we heard her shuffling down the hallway. Then she would peek through the window to make sure it was actually us. Once we figured that out, we started standing there staring directly back through the glass waiting on her face to appear. At one point the lock was backwards and we learned how to tear up a credit card and get ourselves inside anyway.
We did not care if she felt like having company.
We were coming in.
And then somewhere along the way, the woman who used to hide when we arrived became the woman who never wanted us to leave. “I love you more than anything in this world,” she would tell us. She said it all the time.
I believe her.
The older I got, though, the more complicated my own feelings toward her became. I became closer with her children. I heard things I had never understood when I was little. I saw their hurt. I learned about mistakes Mawmaw had made long before I was old enough to know her as anything other than the person who made me feel safe. For a while, I let that replace too much of what I knew about her.
I held resentment. I stopped visiting as much.
I regret that deeply.
I thought growing up meant seeing people clearly, but for a while I confused seeing somebody’s flaws with seeing the whole person. Mawmaw had real flaws. She could be selfish, dramatic, stubborn, manipulative, difficult, and exhausting. She could hurt people she loved.
She could also make me feel safer than anybody in the world.
Both were true.
She was never just one thing. None of us are.
I think grief has made me meet her again in a way I could not while she was alive. I see the little girl in Gattman. The fifteen-year-old mother. The beautiful redheaded woman. The mother her children sometimes needed more from. The grandmother who thought we hung the moon. The stubborn old woman who still believed nothing and nobody was the boss of Barbara.
Toward the end of her life, she worried a lot about getting old. She talked about her wrinkles and bought wrinkle creams like one of them might eventually figure out how to turn back time. Sometimes she would look at herself and say she was ashamed she had “let herself get like this.” We hated when she said that.
She was looking in the mirror and seeing an old woman.
We were looking at Mawmaw.
She had spent so much of her life being beautiful and girly and noticed that I think she still felt like that young redheaded woman inside. Then she would look in the mirror and see someone older staring back at her. We wanted her to understand that we did not care. She was still beautiful because she was ours.
Mawmaw had always seemed so strong that it took us a long time to accept her weakness. For years she told us something was wrong and sometimes we rolled our eyes. She liked attention. She exaggerated things. She could be too sick to do something when it was convenient and then find energy for something she wanted to do.
Then eventually something really was wrong.
And it was hard to understand.
Mawmaw was not supposed to be weak. She was the bull. She did not move for snakes. She did not back down. It felt wrong to watch someone who had always seemed bigger than her body become fragile inside it.
And so much of that happened in the same room where we had always watched her live. Her bedroom had been the center of everything for years. The TV was on. Her things were piled around her. Somebody was always coming in or out. She sang from that bed. She laughed there. She fussed at us there. She watched her shows there. We climbed into bed beside her, sat in chairs, stood in the doorway, brought her Dr Pepper, listened to the same stories again.
Then, at the end, that became the room where we watched her die.
I helped my mom scoot her higher in the bed. I helped give her medicine when her fever was high. I went looking for a breathing treatment machine because I wanted anything that might help her breathe a little easier.
There was nothing poetic about those moments.
I just wanted my Mawmaw to feel better.
When I was little, I used to have nightmares about her dying. Then I grew up and thought I had gotten old enough to understand that people die and that one day I would lose her.
I had not.
            When it happened, it felt exactly like the nightmare.
I still cannot go back into that room.
For a long time I thought that room was comforting because of the bed and the heating blanket and the television and the familiar light coming through the windows. I thought it was the smell of coffee and cigarette smoke and Pledge, or the sound of people coming in and out of the house.
But rooms are only rooms.
It was her.
She was the warmth in it. She was the noise. She was the person everybody was walking toward when they came through the house.
She was home.
When I picture her now, though, I do not picture her sick. I see her laughing and dancing all fixed up. I see her sitting in the recliner tapping her foot to music. I see her sitting up in bed singing. I see those Dollar General glasses hanging from their little string beside her face. I hear her telling me to pick out a “pretty” at the store. I see her in an old dirty T-shirt sitting beside the lake fishing. I see her perched up on pillows so her tiny self could see well enough to drive. I see her playing with her great-grandchildren.
Mostly, I see the way she looked at us.
Like we were her favorite people in the world.
And now I see her in the rest of us too. I see her when one of the women in our family refuses to back down. I hear her when somebody says something a little too bluntly. I feel her when we laugh at something inappropriate at exactly the wrong time. I understand her every time one of “the fellers” gets listened to instead of us.
We spent years saying, “That’s just Mawmaw.”
Now I realize how much Mawmaw is still just us.
She taught me how to sew and fish and bake, but those are not the things I think about most. She taught me to stand up for myself. She taught me not to take any shit. She taught me how to relax and appreciate small things. She taught me that you can be tough as hell and still get your feelings hurt. Sometimes she taught us by being an example. Sometimes she taught us by being exactly what we did not want to become.
Both mattered.
Mostly, she taught me that a woman could be completely herself. Mawmaw was everything a woman, especially a woman of her generation, was not supposed to be. She was loud when women were supposed to be quiet. She was angry when they were supposed to be pleasant. She was funny, inappropriate, girly, vain, stubborn, emotional, fearless, loving, and sometimes mean.
She was not anybody’s storybook grandmother.
Thank God.
A storybook grandmother would have been easier to explain, but she would not have been nearly as much fun.
There is one gospel song Mawmaw loved that I understand differently now. It is the same song she made me stand beside her and sing in church, the one where she sang her part over and over while I refused to answer and she eventually pinched me hard enough that any reasonable child would have started singing. I still did not.
At the time, I was embarrassed and stubborn. I did not care what the words meant. I only knew that I was not singing in front of everybody.
Now I think maybe I have been learning that song my whole life.
*On this Glory Road I’m traveling,*
*Many times I stumbled on my way.*
*Praise the Lord, I’ll soon be leaving*
*To that land of perfect peace and endless day.*
Many times I stumbled on my way.
If there was ever a line for Barbara, maybe that was it.
Her road was not easy or neat. She stumbled. She made mistakes. She hurt people and got hurt herself. She loved people fiercely without always knowing how to love them gently. She carried pieces of her childhood with her for the rest of her life. She fought. She laughed. She cussed. She sang. She kept going.
And she found joy after a childhood that did not give her enough of it. The little girl who thought Santa hated her grew into the grandmother who filled the house with Christmas lights and horribly wrapped presents. The girl who grew up with almost nothing filled every closet and cabinet with things she could not bear to let go of. The fifteen-year-old mother eventually became Mawmaw to grandchildren and great-grandchildren she believed were better than everybody else’s. The woman who spent her whole life refusing to let anything be the boss finally reached a place where she had to let the people who loved her take care of her.
She stumbled on her way.
So did we.
I certainly did with her.
That is one of the things I wish I could tell her now. I wish I had understood sooner that loving somebody honestly does not mean pretending they never hurt anyone. It means being able to see all of them at once. Barbara was the funny parts and the hard parts. The bull and the big watery eyes. The gospel songs and the cussing. The woman who sometimes hid when we came to visit and the woman who later followed us all the way to the car because she could not stand to watch us leave.
She was all of it.
And we loved all of her, even when we did not know how to do that perfectly either.
I think that is why the last part of that verse means so much to me now: *to that land of perfect peace and endless day.* Peace did not always come easily to Mawmaw here. She fought the world so much that sometimes I think she forgot she was allowed to rest.
I hope she knows now that she does not have to fight anything.
I hope there are no mirrors making her feel old. I hope she does not think about wrinkles or whether she has “let herself get like this.” I hope she is every age she ever loved being. I hope her hair is bright red and teased too big, her rouge is on, and she is wearing something that matches all the way down to the hat. I hope Bobby is there. I hope her daddy is there. I hope somebody handed her an instrument.
I hope it is loud enough for Barbara.
And I hope she finally understands something we could never quite make her understand here: we did not need her to be perfect to love her.
We just needed her to be Mawmaw.
I still tell people I wish they could have met her because stories can only get so close. I can tell them she was funny, but I cannot give them the sound of that little chuckle when she knew she had said something good. I can tell them she could sing, but I cannot make them hear her from the bedroom. I can tell them she was mean, but I cannot explain how somebody could hurt your feelings and somehow make you love her more. I can tell them about the house, but I cannot give them the feeling of walking through it knowing she was somewhere in that room waiting for us, whether she actually wanted company or not.
I can tell them she loved us.
But I cannot make them know what it felt like to have Barbara look at you like you were one of the best things God ever made.
I would still give anything to walk into that room one more time. I would take the complaining. I would take the cussing. I would listen to the same story I had already heard five hundred times. I would let her tell me whatever I wanted to do was “just not going to wooorrrkkk.” I would let her brag about me and improve the details. I would let her tell me she was partial to me. I would even let her pinch me.
And if she started that gospel song again, I would sing this time.
For most of my life I thought home was that house, and then I thought it was that room. Now I know better. Home was the woman inside it, and she left so much of herself in us that I think we will spend the rest of our lives finding pieces of Barbara in one another.
Her road was never perfect. Neither was ours with her.
But God, did we have fun traveling it together.
And now, when I hear that song, I like to imagine that after every hard mile, she finally reached the place she had been singing toward all those years. When I picture the lights of home now, she is part of them—the red hair, the rouge, the music, the laugh, and the woman who loved us more than anything in this world.
***I can see the lights of home.***
