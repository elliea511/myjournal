# Biscuits and Gravy

Most childhood memories fade without notice. A few, though, stay close enough to reach for without even trying. One of the memories I return to most often is making biscuits and gravy with my mawmaw. She passed away in August of 2025, and since then I have often found myself thinking back to the time we spent together in her kitchen. She made the best biscuits and gravy, and all her grandchildren begged for them constantly, me included. I was always quick to volunteer to help because I have loved cooking for as long as I can remember. Looking back, some of the warmest moments of my childhood came from standing beside her while we made biscuits and gravy.

The first thing I remember is how full of life the kitchen felt with her in it. The fluorescent lights would hum before they fully came on, and then a bright white light would spread across the room. Her kitchen had dark wooden cabinets on both sides, and scattered through all that dark wood were her bright red appliances and little decorations. Red was her color. She had bright red hair her entire life, and even when it started to darken as she aged, she would have it dyed right back. So of course, her kitchen matched her. Red toaster, red stovetop covers, red dish towels, red plates, red mixer, red coffee pot, red rugs; if a red version existed, she had it. I remember the cabinet doors opening and closing and her red hair bobbing across the tile floor as she searched for the iron skillet she could never remember where she had put. She was so short that we used to joke we were legally required to put her in a booster seat when we traveled, so she carried a step ladder back and forth across the kitchen constantly. Her cabinets stretched all the way to the ceiling and if she needed something higher than the first shelf, she would just climb onto the counter. I always had to stand behind her like a cheerleader waiting for a flyer to do a flip. I do not know if she realized how nervous it made me, or if she did and just thought it was funny. I never thought much about her little quirks because they were just normal to me. Now, when I see that same kitchen without her, it feels empty, like the room has all the right parts but nothing to put them in motion.

I also remember how the kitchen was always full of different smells and sounds. There was usually a faint burnt smell coming from the oven from something she had spilled before, and every time she preheated it, that smell would come back. If she had made a big mess the last time she cooked, smoke would start filling the air and I would get put on “fanning the smoke detector” duty. Most of the time, my efforts were useless, and once our eyes started to water, we would open the sliding screen door next to the kitchen to air it out. Eventually, that smell would be overpowered by the scent of sausage cooking and the soft smell of flour spread across the counter. Her fat tabby cat would circle around our legs and meow every time we opened the fridge expecting a treat. I never felt inclined to give him one because he was probably the meanest cat I have ever met. The smell of blue Dawn dish soap was always floating around too because my mawmaw, a messy cook, was constantly reminding me that if you “wash as you go, you won’t have anything to do at the end.” I always wanted to tell her that if that were true, she would probably be able to find her skillet without opening every cabinet in the kitchen, but I never did because in her kitchen, there was always something sizzling, slamming, smoking, or meowing.

What I remember most though, is how connected I felt to her in those moments. I always wanted to help, even when I was probably too little to be doing much of anything. By the time I was around eleven I was taller than her, so I got promoted to “tall people duty.” Tall people duty, when you are eleven years old and barely five feet tall, means doing everything she would normally do just slightly safer. She had one of those built-in ovens set above the lower cabinets beside the stovetop instead of underneath it like a normal oven, so I would volunteer to put the iron skillet in even though it felt like my wrist was about to snap from how heavy it was. When I cut out biscuits, I would hold each one up and show her before dropping it in the Crisco she had melted in the oven, waiting for her approval. I stood next to her listening to the scraping of her spoon against the bottom of the pan while she stirred the gravy patiently waiting for it to thicken. When I started taking over more of the bigger steps, like rolling out the dough, she would stand next to me with her head tilted back, looking down her nose through her glasses while she watched. Every time she told me I was doing a good job, I felt so proud. The best moment, though, was when everything was done and she would call out, “Biscuits are ready, come eat,” and everyone would flood into the kitchen excited. She would finally sit down, slightly hunched over from her back aching after all her cheerleading stunts, a light layer of sweat across her freckled face, and just watch us eat.

What I miss is not just making biscuits and gravy with her. I miss the life she brought into that kitchen, the noise and movement that made it feel so full, and the closeness I felt standing beside her. It was never really about the food. It was about being near her and getting to see exactly who she was. Her stubbornness, her humor, her independence, her kindness, and the love she had for her family all showed up in that kitchen. That is why biscuits and gravy will always be my comfort food. She always made them for me, and she was the place I felt most comforted.
